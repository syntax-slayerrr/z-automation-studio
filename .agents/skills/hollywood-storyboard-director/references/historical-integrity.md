# Historical Integrity and Responsible Reconstruction

## 1. Establish an evidence boundary

Before visualizing historical material, separate:

- **Verified fact:** explicitly supported by supplied or verified source material.
- **Source claim:** stated in the provided script or brief but not independently checked.
- **Reasonable contextual reconstruction:** period detail used to make a scene legible, but not evidence of a specific documented event.
- **Dramatization:** invented dialogue, private behavior, character interaction, or specific action used for storytelling.
- **Visual metaphor:** symbolic imagery that represents an idea rather than a literal historical event.
- **Unknown:** details for which there is not enough evidence.

Label these distinctions in the storyboard notes when relevant.

## 2. Never manufacture authenticity

Do not invent or confidently specify:
- exact clothing, insignia, flags, logos, banners, or organization marks;
- architectural details or room layouts;
- exact documents, handwriting, meeting minutes, or printed headlines;
- named participants, attendance, dialogue, or gestures;
- exact dates, routes, counts, or locations;
- camera-like archival details (grain, lens, damage) that imply a generated image is an authentic archival photograph.

When details are unknown, use a restrained generic period treatment and mark it as a reconstruction. Do not add false precision.

## 3. Preserve historical chronology

Track dates, places, organizations, titles, and sequence of events. If the supplied material appears inconsistent, flag it rather than silently fixing it. Do not rearrange chronology for drama without explicitly stating the editorial choice and its implications.

## 4. Represent real people responsibly

Do not invent private thoughts or present imagined dialogue as quotations. If dramatized dialogue is requested, label it as fictionalized. When no reliable visual reference exists, use generalized representation rather than presenting a specific generated face as a verified likeness.

## 5. Archival material versus generated illustration

Clearly distinguish:
- actual archival photographs / documents supplied by the production;
- licensed or verified reference material;
- generated reconstruction;
- graphic maps, diagrams, and symbolic images.

Do not prompt generated imagery to imitate a real archival photograph so convincingly that the audience could mistake it for evidence. Where a historical reconstruction is used, the final production may need an on-screen or caption disclosure depending on editorial standards.

## 6. Source and verification log

For every material historical visual detail, record its basis:

| Detail | Basis | Confidence | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Date / place / event | Supplied text or source | High / medium / low | Preserve / verify |
| Clothing / object / architecture | Reference or reconstruction | High / medium / low | Confirm / simplify |
| Dialogue / private action | Source or dramatization | Verified / fictionalized | Label / remove |

If the production brief provides no sources, say that historical verification remains the user's responsibility or request sources when the uncertain detail materially affects the image.
