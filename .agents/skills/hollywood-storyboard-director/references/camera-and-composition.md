# Camera, Composition, and Blocking

## 1. Shot scale

Choose shot scale based on what the audience needs to read:

- **Extreme wide / extreme long:** geography, isolation, scale, social context, or a transition across time.
- **Wide / long:** full-body action, group relationships, movement through space, and environmental context.
- **Medium wide / medium:** interaction, gesture, and readable performance with context.
- **Medium close-up:** facial response and interpersonal tension while retaining some spatial context.
- **Close-up:** a specific emotional beat or essential detail.
- **Extreme close-up / insert:** a detail whose meaning matters—hands, an object, a document mark, a mechanical action, or a small reaction.

Do not use a close-up simply to make a moment feel important. The audience must understand why this detail matters.

## 2. Camera angle and height

- **Eye-level:** direct, neutral, relational.
- **Low angle:** can suggest power, resolve, threat, or vulnerability when the subject is overwhelmed by the frame; avoid automatic hero worship.
- **High angle:** can reveal spatial pattern, isolation, vulnerability, or institutional scale; avoid using it as a generic “weakness” signal.
- **Overhead:** useful for spatial design, collective patterns, documents, maps, or symbolic geometry.
- **Dutch angle:** use sparingly for meaningful instability or disorientation, not as decoration.
- **POV / subjective framing:** use when audience alignment with a character or observer materially matters.

Specify camera height and viewing direction when a vague label would leave the artist uncertain.

## 3. Lens impression

Focal length is not a magic mood label. When useful, describe the *impression*:
- **Wide-angle impression:** stronger spatial depth, expanded environment, possible perspective exaggeration; useful for immersive spaces and dynamic movement.
- **Normal / natural impression:** balanced proportions and familiar spatial relationships; useful for grounded interaction.
- **Long-lens impression:** compressed depth, isolated subject, layered background; useful for observation, distance, or visual compression.
- **Macro / close-focus impression:** tactile attention to a small object or surface.

Give an actual focal length only when the production pipeline or user requires it. Otherwise, describe the intended visual behavior to avoid false precision.

## 4. Camera movement must be motivated

- **Static / locked-off:** confidence, observation, tension, clarity, or allowing performance to carry the moment.
- **Pan / tilt:** redirect attention or reveal information within a fixed spatial relationship.
- **Dolly / track:** move through space or change the audience's relationship to a subject.
- **Push-in:** increase intimacy, emphasis, or pressure; use only when the emotional progression supports it.
- **Pull-back:** reveal context, isolation, consequence, or a changed relationship to the environment.
- **Crane / vertical move:** reveal scale, geography, or a shift in perspective.
- **Handheld impression:** instability, immediacy, or subjective presence; do not apply randomly.
- **Orbit:** reveal spatial relationships or emotional connection; ensure the geography remains understandable.
- **Whip / rapid move:** transfer attention or energy; only when the edit and action justify it.
- **Parallax move:** create layered depth in a 2.5D / hybrid production; define layer separation and avoid excessive fake depth.

For every moving camera, specify start state, end state, direction, speed / easing, and the reason for the move. If these cannot be described, consider a static camera.

## 5. Blocking and screen direction

For every shot with people or moving subjects, define:
- subject positions in frame and in the scene;
- movement path and destination;
- eye-line and who / what is being observed;
- screen direction (left-to-right, right-to-left, toward camera, away from camera);
- foreground / background relationships;
- whether the camera crosses the established axis.

Maintain the **180-degree principle** when spatial clarity matters. Establish the axis of action, keep opposing subjects on consistent screen sides, and preserve eye-line direction. Cross the axis only with a motivated transition: show the camera crossing, use a neutral shot, re-establish geography, or clearly reset the scene.

For groups, define a simple spatial map or named positions when the exact arrangement matters. Avoid vague directions such as “the crowd moves naturally.”

## 6. Focus and depth

Specify focus only when it guides attention:
- subject in sharp focus, environment softened;
- deep focus to preserve relationships across layers;
- rack focus from object to person or vice versa;
- selective focus for a reveal.

A focus change should have a clear start and end target. Do not prescribe rack focus as a generic cinematic effect.

## 7. Camera and edit relationship

A camera move may complete within a shot, lead into a cut, or continue across a match cut. State the intended edit point. Avoid designing movement that conflicts with the next shot's screen direction or rhythm.

## 8. Lens and camera language for generated imagery

If image generation is used, translate the desired camera behavior into a still-frame prompt. A still image cannot communicate a full camera move by itself. Add a separate motion instruction for video / animation tools, including start frame, end frame, camera path, speed, and what remains stationary.
