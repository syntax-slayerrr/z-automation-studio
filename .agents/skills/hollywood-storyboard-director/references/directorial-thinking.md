# Directorial Thinking

## 1. Direct the experience, not the camera vocabulary

A director's primary question is not “Which camera move looks cool?” It is “What should the audience experience at this exact moment?” Camera, blocking, performance, lighting, sound, and editing are tools serving that experience.

Before designing a shot, complete this sentence:

> At this moment, the audience should notice / feel / understand ______ because ______.

If the blank cannot be completed, the shot is probably not designed yet.

## 2. Find the dramatic engine

For each scene or sequence, identify:
- **Objective:** what the character, narrator, or scene is trying to achieve.
- **Obstacle:** what makes that difficult, uncertain, or emotionally charged.
- **Stakes:** what changes if the objective succeeds or fails.
- **Turn:** the new information, decision, action, or feeling that changes the scene.
- **Aftertaste:** the idea or emotion that should remain as the scene ends.

In a historical explainer, the “character” may be a generation, a movement, a community, or an institution rather than one protagonist. Find the human consequence behind the historical fact.

## 3. Design an emotional progression

Map the sequence's feeling from start to end. Example patterns include:

- curiosity → context → tension → discovery;
- uncertainty → pressure → collective action → resolve;
- distance → human detail → empathy → reflection;
- stability → disruption → adaptation → new possibility.

Do not force a three-act template on every short scene. Use the progression that fits the material.

## 4. Translate information into drama

For each factual or explanatory beat, ask:
1. Does the audience need to see the fact itself, its consequence, or the people affected by it?
2. Can a prop, location, gesture, spatial contrast, or visual change carry the information?
3. Would literal illustration be redundant with narration?
4. Is a pause, reaction, or reveal more powerful than another explanatory image?

Avoid “narration says a school, therefore show a generic school” as an automatic rule. Show the specific function of that school in the story: who enters, what changes, what is at stake, or what the place represents.

## 5. Visualize subtext without inventing history

A visual metaphor can enrich a historical sequence, but it must not be mistaken for archival evidence. Label symbolic or reconstructed imagery in production notes. Keep the visual metaphor honest to the historical context and avoid implying that an illustrative scene is a documented event.

## 6. Every shot has a job

Useful shot functions:
- **Establish:** orient the audience in place, time, or social scale.
- **Identify:** introduce a person, group, object, or institution.
- **Reveal:** disclose information progressively.
- **Emphasize:** make a detail or consequence unmistakable.
- **Connect:** establish a relationship between people, places, or ideas.
- **Contrast:** show a meaningful difference.
- **React:** allow the audience to read a response.
- **Transition:** move across time, place, subject, or emotional state.
- **Resolve:** give a visual endpoint or thematic echo.

A shot may serve two functions, but it should have one dominant purpose.

## 7. Directorial restraint

Use spectacle only when it changes the audience's experience. A large camera move, dramatic backlight, slow motion, or extreme close-up is not automatically powerful. A still frame with precise staging can be more compelling.

A director's notes should be actionable, not self-congratulatory. Replace “make it epic” with “hold the wide composition as the crowd moves in one direction, then cut to the single figure who does not move; the contrast makes the decision readable.”
